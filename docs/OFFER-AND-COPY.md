## 7. HONEST CAVEATS

### Four things the owner must confirm before this ships

1. **The prices.** $1,500 / $6,500 / $14,000 / $1,800-per-month / $150-per-hour are anchored on the only directly comparable published competitor ladder (Notionalize: diagnostic from $3.5k, fixed-scope project from $5k, partnership from $2k/mo) and set above it because ClarityOps holds far more proof. **They are not derived from ClarityOps' own closed invoices, which I don't have.** Reconcile against your last ten closed projects. A published floor below your real delivery cost is worse than no floor and nearly impossible to walk upward. If your real numbers differ, change the numbers — the structure still holds.
2. **The Manual-Step Commitment is the only promise with real labour exposure.** It is safe only if "eliminate" is marked step-by-step on a ledger the client signs at blueprint stage, and only if the four-week cap and the client SLA are in the contract. The SLA (access by day 2, feedback in 48 hours) also only protects you if you actually invoke it when a client goes quiet — which small firms hate doing. Decide now who sends that email.
3. **The published ceilings become expectations the day they go live.** 7 databases / 10 automations / 8 platforms / 1 agent / 1 reporting surface / 650 records are each at or under a shipped build, which is why they're defensible — but every Sprint must be deliverable at that ceiling at $14,000, or the Fixed-Price Commitment breaks on the first hard build. The Sprint Scope Policy and the overage mechanism must exist as real documents before any number is published.
4. **The Blueprint needs machinery before it's advertised.** A payment link, a five-day delivery cadence, a reusable ledger template, a repeatable Miro architecture format, and a written 90-day credit policy. Advertising a paid product you improvise will produce the worst first impression for the buyers most ready to pay.

### Claims I removed because the proof can't carry them

- **"10K+ hours saved"** is not used anywhere load-bearing in this copy. It is a round number with a plus — the signature of an estimate — and it cannot survive "how did you calculate that?" on a call. Either reconstruct it from real builds, or leave it off. I kept 300+ businesses and 1,200+ automations because they are plausibly countable; if they aren't, split the claim honestly ("300+ businesses advised · N full systems built") rather than inventing precise-looking substitutes. **Do not publish fabricated precision** like "1,247 automations" or "10,400 hours" — precision invites the follow-up question at peak scrutiny, and a pause there kills the page.
- **Any hours-saved, revenue or ROI promise** for a future client. No such asset exists, and the existing "14 hrs/week handed back to you" on the site is from a decorative mock. Deleted, not repurposed.
- **Any dollar figure attributed to a client outcome.** The only one on the current site ($32K in overdue invoices) is fictional.
- **"70% of transformations fail."** Traces to a 1993 book whose authors called it an unscientific estimate. I also dropped the BCG 30/44/26 substitute: enterprise-transformation data (70 companies, 825 executives) has no message-to-market match with a 5–50 person team, and citing it invites the buyer to say so out loud.
- **SaaS as an industry tile.** Five industries are claimed, four have case studies, SaaS has none. The tile would manufacture an expectation inside the section built to demonstrate fit. Get the case study or leave it out.
- **"French-native delivery"** is real and rare, so it stays — but publishing it creates an expectation of French delivery on demand. Only keep it if you can still honour it.

### What the research could not validate

- **Price transparency data is B2B SaaS, not services.** HockeyStack's 31M-visitor dataset (hidden pricing: more form fills at 4.6% vs 2.8%; transparent pricing: submit-to-pipeline 17.50% vs 10.31%) almost certainly transfers in *direction* to a consultancy. The absolute rates are not benchmarks to expect.
- **No controlled study isolates guarantee presence for high-ticket B2B systems consulting.** The commitment stack rests on the published practice of credible firms and on the logic of the value equation — treat it as a testable hypothesis, not a settled result.
- **Unverifiable stats excluded on purpose:** the "32% higher win rates from risk-reversal language" figure (absent from the body of its own cited source), "money-back guarantees lift sales 21%", testimonial-specificity percentages, and the specific figures from HBR's paywalled good-better-best article. The underlying principles are sound; the percentages aren't citable.
- **Competitor price anchors are partly unconfirmed.** Notionalize's ladder is verified from their live pricing page. Agency Supply's reported $2,000–$6,500+ came from search snippets behind an HTTP 403 and should not be used as a positioning anchor without checking it directly.
- **One inherited claim in the audit is wrong and shouldn't drive the build estimate.** The audit asserts a fully-styled `.case-card__result` component exists at `styles.css:3004`; in the actual file only the section comment `/* ─── Case studies: result stat + tags ─── */` is there — no rule. Likewise the OS kicker names *are* rendered, but only inside the modal header (`script.js` ~line 505), not on the cards. The work is still right; just budget it as real front-end work rather than wiring up something already built.

### Two measurement conditions, agreed before launch

- **The metric is closes per call and revenue per 1,000 visitors — not booking count.** Publishing price will reduce bookings. That is the intended trade. If success is judged on booking volume, this will be abandoned at week three for the wrong reason.
- **The capacity line must be updated monthly and must be true.** The site has already been caught with a stale "Now booking Q3 builds." An obviously evergreen scarcity claim is worse than none.
